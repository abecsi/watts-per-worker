Watts per Worker

A chatbot answer costs less electricity than a few seconds of a light bulb. However, using AI agent for a white-collar job is a different case. What would it take to give one to every office worker on Earth? Would it be realistic to expect that AI can take over all office jobs?

We keep reading and hearing about the threat AI poses to jobs, especially office jobs, and what that would mean for society. I wanted to find out whether such widespread use of AI would even be physically possible. This is the angle we rarely read about, even though it sets the limits of what is feasible. So let's look at the numbers.

We can start with two figures. A chatbot answer burns about 0.3 Wh of electricity, roughly what a light bulb uses in a few seconds. One AI agent, measured while it worked through a real task with tools, used 348 Wh. That's a thousand times more. And that gap, more than any chip or model launch, decides what "AI for every office worker" would actually mean. I'm not forecasting lost jobs here, but asking a blunter question: if AI did the work, what would the machines need?

Most debates about AI and energy start at one of two extremes. Either a single prompt, which looks harmless, or a whole national grid, which looks overwhelming. The interesting part sits in between, and it's just arithmetic: how many tasks, by how many people, at how many watt-hours each. The inputs are public now, so we can run the sums ourselves, for the more than one billion knowledge workers that Gartner counts worldwide,5 in a world where the ILO finds one in four jobs exposed to generative AI.6 The answer turns out to hinge on one choice: whether the AI answers our questions or does the work.

01

Cheap prompts but expensive tasks

Google says its median Gemini text prompt costs 0.24 Wh, idle chips and cooling included.2 A 2026 study in Joule by Oviedo and colleagues lands in the same neighborhood, 0.31 Wh for a standard answer of about 300 output tokens. If we let the model think through 5,000 tokens, though, the bill climbs to 3.91 Wh, thirteen times more.3 More tokens, more energy. Simple as that.

Agents consume more. A KAIST team ran agents built on a 70-billion-parameter model through real tool-using tasks and clocked 348.41 Wh per task on average, 136.5 times a simple question-answering run on the same setup, while the GPUs sat idle for up to 54.5% of the time, waiting on the tools to answer.4 That's a research rig, not a polished commercial service, so we should treat it as a ceiling. But it shows us the shape of the problem plainly enough. An agent loops. It calls a tool, rereads its own context, tries again, and every lap costs watt-hours.

The gray bar is the useful comparison. A human brain runs on roughly 20 watts,10 so a full hour of thinking costs about 20 Wh. One measured agent task costs seventeen of those hours. The gap is the reason a per-prompt number reassures and a per-job number alarms.

02

Now multiply by a billion desks

The model below is deliberately simple: workers × tasks per day × energy per task × working days, with an optional factor for data-center overhead and one for efficiency gains. We can choose a preset, then move the sliders. Every output updates against the same fixed yardsticks: all data centers on Earth in 2024 (415 TWh), the IEA's 2030 and 2035 projections, and total global electricity demand in 2025 (31,779 TWh).1,7

If we run the presets in order, the story tells itself. If we give a billion people an assistant that answers 30 questions a day, we need about 2.3 TWh a year. Data centers already used 415 TWh in 2024. They'd barely feel it. If we swap those for 100 long reasoning answers per person, demand climbs to about 98 TWh, which sounds like a lot, yet still fits inside what the industry is already building.

Agents break the pattern. Twenty agent tasks a day, at the measured 348 Wh, add up to roughly 1,740 TWh a year, more than the IEA's most aggressive 2035 case for all data centers combined, from this single use. If we let those agents run around the clock as a fleet, the total passes half of the world's electricity. No grid is planning for that, and none could build it this decade.

Twenty agent tasks a day for a billion workers would use more electricity than every data center on Earth in the IEA's highest 2035 case.

Is efficiency the escape hatch? Partly. Google says the energy of its median prompt fell 33-fold in twelve months,2 and Oviedo and colleagues see another eight- to ninefold savings within reach. If we apply 8.5× to the agent case, it falls to about 205 TWh, roughly half of today's data-center total. That said, cheaper tasks have a habit of multiplying. When we make something cheap, we do more of it, an old story in energy, and the tokens-per-task curve is rising faster than the energy-per-token curve is falling.

03

Electricity is the easy part to count

A terawatt-hour is just an abstract number, but what gets built is a turbine, a transformer, a rack and a building, and each has its own queue.

We can start with the machines. One rack-scale system of the GB200 NVL72 class holds 72 GPUs, draws about 120–132 kW and weighs 1.36 tonnes.8 The agent preset implies an average load of about 199 GW. At full utilization, that is roughly 1.5 million such racks, over 100 million GPUs and two million tonnes of rack hardware alone, before a single building, chiller or cable tray. Real fleets never run at full load, so the true count would be higher.

Then the power. GE Vernova, the largest gas-turbine maker, had roughly 10 GW of production slots left across 2029 and 2030 combined after its first quarter of 2026, with data centers making up about a fifth of its 100 GW under contract.9 Large power transformers in the US average about 128 weeks to deliver, and the biggest units three to five years.11 A scenario that needs 199 GW of new, steady supply is asking for about twenty times the slots the largest turbine maker had left for 2029 and 2030, unless it comes from wind, solar, storage and nuclear, which have queues of their own.

The campuses take one to three and a half years from bare land to first power. The equipment that feeds them can take as long on its own, and has to be ordered first. In Concrete and Compute, one of the fastest timelines belonged to Colossus 2, where xAI ran its own gas turbines on site and secured the air permit for 41 of them only afterwards. That shortcut works for one campus. It does not scale to a billion AI co-workers.

04

Where it would sit, and what it is made of

Electricity has to come from somewhere, and the halls that use it have to stand somewhere. We can use two recent campuses as a handy yardstick: Stargate Abilene puts 1.2 GW on about 875 acres, and Meta's Hyperion plans 5 GW on 3,200 acres, so a gigawatt of AI capacity needs roughly 2.6 to 3 square kilometers of land.15 If we scale that to the agent scenario, at about 199 GW, we get around 560 km² of campuses, the footprint of a large city.

Here's the twist, though. The halls are the small part. If all of those 1,740 TWh came from solar farms, NREL's measured land use of 3.5 acres per gigawatt-hour a year puts the panels, roads and substations on 21,000 to 28,000 km².16 That's about the size of New Jersey, and it doesn't include a single battery to carry the load through the night. Gas plants would need far less land, but they need turbines the factories have already sold. Either way, the map below makes one point plainly: the question of AI at work is also a land-use question.

The best public reference we have is the ICEF's model of a 200 MW data center: about 300,000 cubic meters of concrete for the whole site, within a range of 55,000 to 500,000, plus roughly 275 tonnes of steel for every megawatt.17 If we scale it up, we land near 720 million tonnes of concrete and 55 million tonnes of steel. Huge numbers. Still, each is only about 3% of a single year of world cement and steel output, because those are colossal industries.

Copper is a different story. At the contested 20 to 70 tonnes per megawatt, a spread wide enough to tell us nobody really knows, the halls alone would swallow 4 to 14 million tonnes, somewhere between a sixth and three-fifths of a year of global mine production.12,18 Concrete fills the chart. Copper is what could actually run out.

05

The materials nobody agrees on

Some inputs are hard to count, and the disagreement is itself a finding. Copper is the clearest case. Industry estimates for AI data centers range from 20–30 tonnes per megawatt for conventional halls to 40–70 for dense liquid-cooled AI halls,12 while a widely shared claim of half a million tonnes per gigawatt for NVIDIA's next rack design turned out to be a units slip. The rack architecture itself needs about 200 kg per megawatt.13 Whether a figure counts the rack, the building or the grid connection moves the answer by two orders of magnitude, so the electricity model leaves copper out, and Exhibit 6 shows it as a range rather than a single number.

Critical mineral
Gallium is easier to pin down, and more uncomfortable. China supplies about 99% of the world's refined gallium, and the IEA estimates data-center demand for it could pass 10% of today's supply by 2030.1 A metal most people have never heard of sits under the chips, the power electronics and the scenario as a whole.
06

So, could it happen?

An AI assistant for every knowledge worker? Realistic today. The energy is trivial, the hardware exists, and each answer keeps getting cheaper. An AI agent doing a full share of every office job, at today's measured energy per task, is another matter entirely. It would need a grid the size of a continent's, built in years that equipment makers have already sold. The largest gas-turbine maker had about 10 GW of production slots left for 2029 and 2030 combined. Big transformers take two and a half to five years to arrive. We can't download either.

The workable path runs somewhere in between, and it's narrow. Efficiency gains do some of the lifting. Agents get used where they earn their watt-hours, not everywhere. And someone orders turbines and transformers years before the model that needs them is even trained. So the real limit on AI at work isn't clever code. It's physical: turbines, transformers, copper, a metal refined almost entirely in one country, and the order books of a handful of factories.

What this model cannot tell you
Per-task energy figures come from different setups: Google's from its own fleet including overhead, Oviedo's from modeled frontier serving, KAIST's from a 70-billion-parameter research system. They are not strictly comparable, and the agent figure in particular reflects one workload on one setup. The model also leaves out training, networks, user devices and the energy of the human work the AI would replace. We should read every output as an order of magnitude.
